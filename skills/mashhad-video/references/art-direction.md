# Art direction: make a film, not a demonstration of tools

Read for a new visual direction or a substantial redesign. For a small revision, retain the established direction and touch only the affected decisions. These are craft heuristics, not mandatory style rules.

## Give the piece one visual argument

Write a sentence connecting subject, emotion, and visual behavior: “A scattered workload becomes a calm, navigable system; scattered fragments progressively share a grid.” This is more actionable than “premium, cinematic, stunning.” Name the intended audience response and the image that earns it. A tutorial may prioritize comprehension; an identity film may prioritize atmosphere.

Choose references for distinct reasons: composition, type, material, pacing, camera. Record what to borrow at the principle level. Avoid combining entire competing visual identities. Preserve the user's references and brand assets. A dark palette, energetic spring, grain, neon, or maximalism can be appropriate when motivated by the brief.

## Resolve the decisive frames first

For a new substantial piece, compose representative styleframes with real copy and near-final assets: the first meaningful image, a difficult information-dense moment, and the payoff as useful. Test the intended output crop, including small-screen viewing. A short existing scene might need only one changed frame.

Make a compact visual system: type roles and actual weights, spacing relationships, palette roles, shape language, texture/material, lighting, and movement character. Match Arabic and Latin type by optical presence rather than equal numeric font size. Make secondary information visibly secondary. Use empty space to prepare an entrance or preserve a focal point, rather than filling every area.

## Storyboard an attention path

Each shot needs: time range, audience takeaway, focal object, action, incoming/outgoing continuity, and any sound or spoken cue. Plan timing from meaning and reading time. The default sound palette is sparse effects and nonmusical atmosphere; silence is an intentional option. Add music only when explicitly requested. For example:

| Shot intent | Staging | Continuity |
|---|---|---|
| Show overload | Similar cards compete near the edges; one item becomes readable | Keep that item's identity |
| Show organization | The same item settles into a clear workflow | Preserve its color and anchor |
| Prove benefit | Reveal the resulting state or honest measured value | Hold long enough to understand |

Do not make every shot a headline over a new background. Change scale and density with purpose: establish context, isolate the important relationship, reveal the result. A cut can communicate a new thought more clearly than an elaborate transition.

## Allocate spectacle

Choose where the strongest motion, camera change, or material reveal belongs. Let quieter moments create contrast. Camera motion should reveal information, connect locations, or change emotional distance; it should not compete with text readers are trying to follow. Assign movement to the object, camera, or typography first; combine them only when attention remains legible.

Materials should explain something tangible: frosted glass separates layers, rough paper supports archival context, polished metal reveals form through lighting. Shader detail needs scale and temporal stability. Check whether highlights, grain, aberration, and blur survive compression without hiding the subject.

Before expanding the film, render its hardest representative moment at practical quality. Use the result to revise the visual argument, type scale, or material budget. An attractive still establishes a direction; temporal review establishes whether it works as a film.
